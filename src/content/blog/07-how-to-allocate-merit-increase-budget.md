A company may want to reward strong performance, retain critical skills and address salary gaps, but its available compensation budget is limited. The difficult question is not simply how much to spend. It is how to allocate the available amount in a way that is consistent, explainable and aligned with the company’s priorities.

### Quick answer: What is the fairest way to allocate a merit budget?

Start with a clear compensation philosophy and eligibility rules. Use consistent performance evidence, salary-range position and documented business considerations to guide recommendations. Model the total cost before decisions are finalized, review exceptions for fairness and keep other pay actions, such as promotions or market corrections, visible as separate budget items.

### Step 1: Calculate the available pool

Assume eligible employees have a combined annual base-payroll total of $10 million and the company approves a merit pool of 3% of that payroll.

**Illustrative merit pool = $10,000,000 × 3% = $300,000.**

This is a planning illustration, not a benchmark recommendation. Confirm which employees and salary components are included. Also decide whether the pool includes employer payroll costs or only base-salary increases.

### Step 2: Separate the reasons for pay changes

Before allocating the merit pool, identify items that should be planned separately. Depending on company policy, these may include:

- Promotion increases for employees moving into larger roles.
- Market adjustments for roles that have become difficult to recruit for.
- Equity corrections following a structured review.
- Statutory or contractual pay changes.
- Retention actions approved outside the normal cycle.

If all of these are hidden inside one merit percentage, the company may unintentionally reduce the money available for performance-based rewards or misstate the purpose of its spending.

### Step 3: Define the decision factors

A practical allocation framework may consider four dimensions:

| Factor | Question HR should ask | Possible evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Performance | What contribution did the employee demonstrate? | Goals, outcomes, role-specific measures and manager evidence |
| Salary position | Where is current pay within the relevant range? | Compa-ratio or range penetration |
| Role and market | Has the job or market reference materially changed? | Job evaluation and reliable market data |
| Equity and consistency | Are comparable employees being treated consistently? | Pay analysis, prior decisions and documented exceptions |

The organization should decide in advance how much weight each factor carries. Do not adjust weights simply to justify a preferred outcome after the recommendations are visible.

### Step 4: Use a merit matrix as a guide, not an automatic answer

A merit matrix can connect performance categories with salary-range position. For example, a company may create different increase guidelines for employees who are below midpoint, near midpoint or above midpoint, and who have different performance ratings.

The exact percentages must be calibrated to the organization’s budget, ranges and reward philosophy. A matrix is not fair simply because it is a table. It must be based on meaningful criteria, tested against the employee population and reviewed for unintended disparities.

### Step 5: Simulate the total cost

Build more than one scenario before publishing the rules. Test what happens if:

- More employees qualify for the highest performance category than expected.
- A department has a different performance distribution from the company average.
- Employees below the midpoint receive additional progression consideration.
- A proposed threshold changes the number of eligible employees.
- A set of market or equity adjustments must be funded separately.

Compare the projected total with the approved pool. If the scenario exceeds the budget, revisit the policy before managers communicate expected outcomes. Do not quietly reduce awards after employees have been assessed against published criteria.

### Step 6: Review exceptions and employee-group outcomes

Ask managers to justify recommendations outside the guidelines. Look for repeated exceptions by manager, department or employee group. Review differences using legally appropriate data and consult local counsel where necessary. A budget can be within limits and still produce inconsistent or unfair outcomes.

### Step 7: Explain the outcome

Employees should understand what influenced their decision, what policy was applied and what they can work toward in the next cycle. Avoid promising that a particular performance score guarantees a fixed future salary increase unless the company’s policy truly provides that guarantee.

### Frequently asked questions

**Should every employee receive the same percentage increase?**  
Not necessarily. Equal percentages can ignore differences in performance, role, salary-range position and other relevant factors. Whatever method is used should be clearly defined and applied consistently.

**Should high performers always receive the largest increase?**  
Performance is important, but it should be considered alongside range position, market competitiveness, internal equity and the company’s stated pay policy.

**What if employee recommendations exceed the approved budget?**  
Pause before communicating final outcomes. Recheck eligibility, assumptions and proposed adjustments, then revise the scenario through a documented approval process. Avoid changing the rules after the cycle has been published.

**Should a company use the entire merit budget?**  
Not automatically. The goal is to make justified decisions within the approved policy, not to spend every dollar for its own sake. Any unused amount should be handled according to the organization’s budget rules.
