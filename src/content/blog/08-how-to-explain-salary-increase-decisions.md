The compensation decision may be complete, but the employee experience is not. An employee who receives a raise may still want to know how it was calculated. Someone who receives a smaller increase may wonder what they could have done differently. An employee who receives no increase may leave the conversation believing the process was arbitrary.

Managers need more than a percentage and a letter. They need a clear explanation of the decision and a practical way to discuss it.

### Quick answer: How should managers explain a salary increase?

Managers should explain the outcome, the criteria used, the employee’s relevant evidence and the next steps. They should be transparent about what influenced the decision, avoid comparing confidential salaries and never promise a future increase that has not been approved.

### Prepare managers before announcing results

Before the conversation, provide managers with:

- The employee’s final salary decision and effective date.
- The criteria and policy applied during the cycle.
- The employee’s assessment and the evidence supporting it.
- A clear explanation of how the decision relates to the employee’s role and salary position, where relevant.
- Approved answers to common questions and a route for escalating errors or concerns.

Managers should not discover the rationale while sitting in front of the employee. HR should also make clear which information is confidential and what managers are authorized to share.

### Conversation example 1: Explaining a salary increase

**Manager:** “Your new base salary will be [new salary], effective [date]. This decision was made through our compensation review. We considered the agreed performance criteria, your contribution during the review period and the company’s salary guidelines. Your strongest contributions were [specific examples]. I’d also like to discuss the goals that will help you continue progressing in your role.”

The manager should insert specific, verified evidence rather than generic praise. If the organization uses a rating or salary-range framework, explain it in plain language.

### Conversation example 2: Explaining a smaller-than-expected increase

**Manager:** “I understand that you expected a different outcome. The increase was determined using the same review framework applied across the relevant employee group. The main factors in your decision were [approved factors]. I can walk you through the parts of the assessment and explain what would strengthen your position in the next review. I also want to make sure your questions are recorded and reviewed if something is missing.”

Do not use “budget” as a vague explanation if other factors contributed. If budget constraints were decisive, explain that honestly without suggesting the employee’s contribution was unimportant.

### Conversation example 3: Explaining no salary increase

**Manager:** “There is no base-salary increase for this cycle. I know that may be disappointing, so I want to explain the decision clearly. Under the criteria for this review, [specific reason or policy factor] affected the outcome. Here are the expectations we need to focus on before the next review: [specific actions]. I’ll support you through [resources or check-ins], and we can review progress on [date or period].”

Only use this script when the explanation accurately reflects the documented decision. Do not invent performance concerns to make a budget decision sound more acceptable.

### Conversation example 4: Employee asks, “Why did my colleague receive more?”

**Manager:** “I can explain the criteria and how your own decision was reached, but I cannot discuss another employee’s confidential compensation details. The factors considered in this review include [approved factors]. If you believe relevant information about your role or contribution was missed, let’s document it and ask HR to review it.”

The organization should have a consistent approach to pay transparency and confidentiality that reflects the law in the places where it operates.

### Five rules for better pay conversations

**Be specific.** Use examples of outcomes, responsibilities and agreed criteria. Avoid vague labels such as “not visible enough” without explaining the relevant job expectation.

**Be consistent.** Managers should not offer different explanations for the same policy simply because they have different communication styles.

**Listen before defending.** Employees may have information about their role or responsibilities that was not included in the review.

**Do not promise what is not approved.** Avoid statements such as “You will definitely get 10% next year.” Discuss development goals without guaranteeing a future pay outcome.

**Close with next steps.** Explain how to request a review, correct data, raise a concern or prepare for the next cycle.

### What should HR include in a salary decision letter?

At minimum, confirm the new salary or the outcome, effective date, pay basis and any required contractual details. Depending on local law and policy, the communication may also need to be provided in writing within a specified period. For example, UK Acas guidance says changes to an employee’s main terms must be put in writing within one month. Employers should check the requirements that apply in each jurisdiction.

### Frequently asked questions

**Should managers tell employees the exact percentage increase?**  
They should communicate the employee’s approved outcome accurately. Whether to provide a percentage, salary range information or additional rationale depends on company policy and applicable law.

**Should an employee’s salary increase be linked to performance feedback?**  
It can be, but the organization should explain how performance was measured and how the result affected pay. Some employers separate developmental feedback from the compensation conversation to encourage more open coaching discussions.

**What if an employee disagrees with the decision?**  
Listen, check whether the employee has new or missing information, explain the review or appeal route and document the concern. Do not treat disagreement as misconduct.

**Who should deliver the salary decision?**  
Often the direct manager delivers the conversation, supported by HR guidance. Sensitive or complex cases may require an HR partner or senior leader to participate.
