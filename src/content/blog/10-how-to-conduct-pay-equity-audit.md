A pay report may show that two employees in similar roles earn different salaries. That difference is a starting point for investigation, not an automatic conclusion. HR needs to understand whether the employees perform comparable work, whether relevant job-related factors explain the difference and whether the organization applies those factors consistently.

A pay equity audit gives the organization a structured way to examine those questions.

### Quick answer: What is a pay equity audit?

A pay equity audit is a structured review of employee compensation to identify pay differences between comparable employees or groups, investigate whether those differences have legitimate and consistently applied explanations, and determine whether policy or pay changes are needed. The exact legal analysis depends on the jurisdictions where the organization operates.

### Pay gap and equal pay are related but different

A workforce pay gap is an aggregate comparison between groups. It can be affected by differences in job levels, representation, working hours, tenure and occupational mix. Equal-pay analysis examines whether people doing equal or comparable work are paid consistently after considering factors that the applicable law permits.

A company should not treat one aggregate percentage as proof that every individual pay decision is fair or unfair. The analysis needs the right comparison groups and the right context.

### Step 1: Define the scope and involve the right people

Decide which employee populations, locations, pay components and time periods are included. Determine whether the audit covers base salary only or also bonuses, allowances, overtime, commissions, equity and benefits.

Assign an accountable HR or compensation owner. Involve Finance, data owners and qualified legal counsel early, especially when the review uses protected-characteristic data or may lead to legal remediation.

### Step 2: Clean and validate the workforce data

Bring together the data needed to understand pay decisions:

- Employee identifier and current employment status.
- Job title, job family, level and location.
- Base salary, currency, pay frequency and FTE.
- Variable compensation and relevant benefits, where in scope.
- Tenure and relevant experience, where appropriate.
- Performance information and documented pay decisions.
- Other job-related factors permitted by policy and applicable law.

Check for duplicate employee records, missing salary values, inconsistent job titles, outdated levels, incorrect FTE values and mixed currencies. Document exclusions and assumptions. Poor data can create misleading comparisons.

### Step 3: Group comparable work

Job titles alone are not enough. Compare job content, responsibilities, required skills, decision-making authority, complexity and working conditions. Group roles that are genuinely comparable under the relevant legal and organizational framework.

If two roles have the same title but materially different scope, they may not belong in the same comparison group. Conversely, different titles may involve substantially similar work. Have knowledgeable job owners validate the grouping.

### Step 4: Identify pay differences

Start with descriptive analysis. Compare mean and median pay, salary ranges and distribution by job group and relevant employee categories. Where appropriate, examine starting salaries, increases, promotions, bonuses and other compensation elements separately.

Then investigate individual or group differences. Statistical analysis can help account for legitimate factors, but the model must be designed carefully. A statistical result is not a substitute for checking job comparability, source data and the actual decision history.

### Step 5: Investigate explanations consistently

For each material difference, ask:

- Is the job genuinely comparable?
- Is the difference related to a documented and permitted factor, such as relevant experience, skill, role scope, performance or location?
- Was that factor applied consistently to other employees?
- Is the documentation contemporaneous and credible?
- Does the explanation account for the size of the observed difference?

Do not assume that a difference is justified simply because a manager can provide a reason after the fact. Equally, do not assume that every difference is unlawful or requires an identical salary outcome.

### Step 6: Build a remediation plan

When a gap cannot be adequately explained, determine the appropriate response with legal and compensation advice. Actions may include salary adjustments, changes to hiring or promotion practices, range corrections, clearer criteria, manager training or improved recordkeeping.

Prioritize actions based on severity, legal risk, employee impact and available budget. Assign owners and deadlines. Do not reduce another employee’s salary as an informal way to make pay figures match; legal requirements vary, and some laws expressly prohibit this approach.

### Step 7: Monitor whether the problem returns

An audit should change the process, not just produce a report. Review starting salaries, promotion decisions, merit increases, off-cycle adjustments and exceptions as part of ongoing governance. Set a repeat review schedule that reflects company size, workforce change and legal requirements.

### Pay equity audit checklist

| Phase | Key deliverable |
|---|---|
| Scope | Written scope, owner, legal review and data-access rules |
| Data | Validated employee and compensation dataset |
| Job grouping | Documented comparable-role methodology |
| Analysis | Descriptive and, where suitable, statistical findings |
| Investigation | Evidence and explanation for material differences |
| Remediation | Prioritized actions, budget, owners and deadlines |
| Monitoring | Recurring controls and follow-up review |

### Frequently asked questions

**How often should a company conduct a pay equity audit?**  
There is no single cadence for every employer. Establish a regular review schedule and repeat the analysis after major reorganizations, acquisitions, compensation-structure changes or other events that could alter pay outcomes. Follow any applicable legal requirements.

**Does a pay gap automatically mean discrimination?**  
No. An aggregate gap can reflect workforce composition and other factors. However, it may signal questions that require investigation. A legal conclusion depends on the applicable law and the facts.

**Can a small company conduct a pay equity audit?**  
Yes. A smaller employer can begin with reliable employee data, sensible job groupings, descriptive comparisons and documented reviews. More complex statistical or legal questions may require specialist support.

**What data should HR include?**  
At minimum, use consistent pay data, job and level information, location and FTE. Add relevant job-related factors and demographic data only where appropriate, lawful and properly protected.

**Should audit findings be shared with employees?**  
The communication approach depends on the findings, legal obligations, privacy and company policy. Leaders should be prepared to explain the organization’s process and the actions it is taking without disclosing other employees’ confidential information.

### Make fairness part of every compensation cycle

A pay equity audit can reveal problems, but prevention depends on everyday decisions. Clear criteria, consistent approvals, employee-level explanations and a reliable audit trail make it easier to understand how compensation outcomes were reached.
