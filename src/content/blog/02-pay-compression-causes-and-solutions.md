A company hires a new employee at the current market rate. The new hire’s salary is only slightly lower than the salary of an employee who has performed the same role successfully for five years. The experienced employee notices. Their manager struggles to explain the difference. HR has a pay-compression problem.

### Quick answer: What is pay compression?

Pay compression occurs when pay differences between employees become unusually small relative to meaningful differences in experience, skills, responsibilities, performance or seniority. It can happen between new hires and experienced employees, or between employees at different job levels.

Pay compression does not automatically prove that a pay decision is unlawful or incorrect. It is a warning sign that the organization should investigate how its pay structure and decisions are working.

### What causes salary compression?

**1. Market salaries move faster than internal salaries.** When an organization raises starting salaries to attract candidates but does not review existing employees’ pay, new hires can approach or overtake the salaries of experienced colleagues.

**2. Salary bands become outdated.** A range that was competitive several years ago may no longer reflect the market for a role or skill set. Hiring managers then make exceptions to close candidates, while current employees remain on older rates.

**3. Promotions are not accompanied by meaningful pay adjustments.** An employee may take on a higher-level role but receive a small increase that leaves their pay close to colleagues in the previous level.

**4. Broad increases ignore differences in pay position.** Giving everyone the same percentage can preserve existing gaps, fail to address under-range pay and leave some employees close to range maximums with little room for progression.

**5. Minimum-wage or market changes affect lower-paid roles.** When the bottom of a pay structure rises, the distance between entry-level and more experienced roles may narrow unless the organization reviews the surrounding structure.

### A simple pay-compression example

Imagine a company has two employees in the same job family:

| Employee | Relevant experience | Monthly salary |
|---|---:|---:|
| New hire | 1 year | LKR 120,000 |
| Experienced employee | 6 years | LKR 126,000 |

The experienced employee earns only LKR 6,000 more, or 5% above the new hire. That difference may or may not be justified. HR should examine role scope, skills, performance, market data, location and the organization’s pay policy before deciding whether an adjustment is appropriate.

The example is illustrative. There is no universal percentage that defines pay compression.

### How can HR identify pay compression?

**Step 1: Compare genuinely comparable jobs.** Job titles alone are not enough. Compare responsibilities, level, decision-making scope, required skills and working conditions.

**Step 2: Review employee pay by level and tenure.** Look for clusters where new hires and experienced employees have very similar pay. Also examine cases where employees in higher-level roles earn close to or below employees at lower levels.

**Step 3: Check salary-range position.** Compare each employee’s salary with the minimum, midpoint and maximum of the relevant range. A range midpoint or compa-ratio can help identify unusual pay positions, but it does not explain the cause on its own.

**Step 4: Review pay decisions over time.** Examine starting salaries, annual increases, promotions, retention adjustments and changes in job scope. Compression often develops through a series of individually understandable decisions.

**Step 5: Check for patterns across groups.** Review the results by function, location and other legally appropriate demographic categories. Protect sensitive data and obtain local legal guidance on the analysis.

### How should a company fix pay compression?

Start with a diagnosis, not a blanket raise. Depending on the facts, the response may include targeted market adjustments, changes to salary ranges, a clearer progression policy, promotion-pay guidelines or a phased correction plan.

Set priorities using documented criteria: business criticality, severity of the gap, range position, role comparability, performance and budget availability. If the company cannot correct every issue immediately, record the risk, assign an owner and establish a review date.

Avoid solving compression for one employee in a way that creates a new unexplained disparity elsewhere. Model the full cost of proposed adjustments before approval.

### How can companies prevent pay compression?

- Review salary structures and market reference points on a planned schedule.
- Define who can approve starting-pay exceptions and require a written reason.
- Establish guidelines for promotion increases and market adjustments.
- Review internal pay relationships during annual compensation planning, not only when someone resigns.
- Document decisions so HR can explain how pay evolved.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is pay compression the same as pay inversion?**  
The terms are related. Pay compression describes unusually small differences between pay levels or employee groups. Pay inversion is a more specific situation in which a lower-level or less-experienced employee earns more than a higher-level or more-experienced employee.

**Should experienced employees always earn more than new hires?**  
Not automatically. Pay should reflect the job, skills, relevant experience, performance, market conditions and the organization’s compensation policy. Tenure alone is not a complete measure of role value.

**Can annual merit increases solve pay compression?**  
They can help when designed to address pay position and internal equity, but a standard merit cycle may not be enough. Some cases require a separate market or equity adjustment.

**How often should companies check for pay compression?**  
Review it as part of the regular compensation-structure review and whenever major hiring, market or organizational changes occur. The appropriate cadence depends on how quickly the labor market and roles change.

### Make compression visible before it becomes a retention problem

When pay decisions live in separate spreadsheets and manager conversations, it is difficult to see how gaps developed. A structured compensation process gives HR and Finance a clearer view of employee-level decisions, budget impact and patterns that deserve review.
