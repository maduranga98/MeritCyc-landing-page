When a company has 20 employees, leaders may know the context behind almost every salary decision. At 150 or 300 employees, that informal approach becomes difficult to maintain. Similar roles can end up with different pay, managers may make inconsistent offers, and employees may not understand how salary progression works.

Salary bands provide a framework for making those decisions more consistently.

### Quick answer: What are salary bands?

A salary band is a defined pay range for a job, job family or level. It normally has a minimum, midpoint and maximum. A set of related bands forms a salary structure or pay-grade framework. The structure helps an organization decide where to hire, how employees can progress and when a salary needs review.

### Step 1: Understand the work before assigning grades

Start by documenting the roles in the organization. Compare the work itself, not only job titles. Consider:

- Core responsibilities and expected outcomes.
- Decision-making authority and business impact.
- Required knowledge, experience and specialist skills.
- People, budget or operational responsibility.
- Complexity, risk and problem-solving expectations.

Group roles with comparable scope into job levels or grades. Avoid creating a separate grade for every employee. Too many narrow grades can make the structure difficult to manage; too few broad grades can hide meaningful differences between roles.

### Step 2: Choose a market and internal-equity approach

External market data helps the company understand what comparable employers pay for similar work. Internal job evaluation helps compare the relative value of roles inside the organization. Many employers use a combination of both.

Before selecting data, define the relevant labor market. A software engineer hired internationally may need a different reference market from a location-bound operations role. Consider geography, industry, organization size, role scope and data quality. Do not mix salary surveys with different definitions of base pay, total cash or benefits without normalizing them.

### Step 3: Set a range minimum, midpoint and maximum

Each band should have three reference points:

- **Minimum:** the lower end of the range for someone who meets the role’s basic requirements but may still be developing in the role.
- **Midpoint:** the reference point the organization uses to represent its target market position or pay policy for a fully capable employee.
- **Maximum:** the upper end of the range for an experienced, capable employee whose pay remains within the role’s established range.

The midpoint is not automatically the correct salary for every person. Individual placement depends on relevant skills, experience, performance, market conditions and the organization’s policy.

A simple illustration:

| Level | Minimum | Midpoint | Maximum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Analyst | $40,000 | $50,000 | $60,000 |
| Senior Analyst | $52,000 | $65,000 | $78,000 |
| Manager | $68,000 | $85,000 | $102,000 |

These figures are illustrative only. There is no universal range spread or midpoint progression that suits every employer.

### Step 4: Write rules for movement within a band

A range is useful only when employees and managers understand how pay progresses through it. Decide what normally influences movement: demonstrated proficiency, sustained performance, relevant skills, expanded responsibilities, certifications or market conditions.

Explain the difference between an increase within the same band and a promotion into a different level. Also define what happens when an employee is already near or above the maximum. Possible approaches include a separate lump-sum award, a market review, a role evaluation or a documented exception process. The right approach depends on policy, local law and the organization’s reward philosophy.

### Step 5: Define governance and exceptions

Document who owns the structure, who can approve exceptions and what evidence is required. Hiring managers should not create permanent exceptions without HR review. Record the reason, approver, effective date and review point for each exception.

Review the structure when roles change materially, when market conditions shift or when repeated exceptions suggest that a band is no longer useful. Avoid changing ranges simply to fit individual salaries without first understanding why those salaries differ.

### Step 6: Explain the structure to employees

A salary structure should not become a secret spreadsheet understood only by HR. Decide what employees and managers can see: job levels, salary ranges, progression criteria and the process for requesting a review. Communicate what the bands mean and what they do not mean. Being within a range does not guarantee a particular increase or promotion.

### Frequently asked questions

**How many salary bands should a company have?**  
There is no fixed number. Build enough levels to reflect meaningful differences in job scope and career progression, but keep the structure simple enough to administer consistently.

**Should every employee in the same grade earn the same salary?**  
Not necessarily. A range allows for differences in proficiency, relevant experience, performance and other defensible factors. The organization should be able to explain those differences consistently.

**How often should salary bands be reviewed?**  
Set a regular review cadence and trigger additional reviews when market conditions, role requirements or business strategy change. Critical or fast-moving job families may need more frequent attention than stable roles.

**What is the difference between a salary band and a pay grade?**  
A pay grade generally groups jobs of comparable internal value or level. A salary band is the pay range associated with a grade or role grouping. Organizations sometimes use the terms differently, so define them in the compensation policy.

### Build a structure that can scale with the business

Salary bands connect job architecture, market positioning and pay progression. They also provide a useful foundation for compensation reviews because HR can understand where each employee sits before allocating an increase.
