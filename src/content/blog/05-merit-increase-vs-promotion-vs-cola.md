Not every salary increase has the same purpose. A high-performing employee may receive a merit increase. An employee moving into a larger role may receive a promotion adjustment. A company may also change salaries because market rates have moved or because a legal minimum has changed.

Treating every change as a standard annual raise can create confusion in policy, payroll and budget reporting.

### Quick answer: What is the difference?

A **merit increase** rewards performance in the employee’s current role. A **promotion raise** adjusts pay when an employee moves into a role with greater scope or responsibility. A **cost-of-living adjustment (COLA)** is intended to respond to changes in living costs or a defined index. A **market adjustment** moves pay toward the organization’s current external market reference. These actions have different triggers and should have clear approval rules.

### 1. Merit increase: Rewarding contribution in the current role

A merit increase is usually linked to an assessment of performance against defined expectations or objectives. The employee remains in the same role or level, but their base salary changes to recognize their contribution.

A credible merit process defines eligibility, performance criteria, rating guidance, budget limits and approval responsibilities before managers submit recommendations. Performance evidence should be consistent enough for decisions to be reviewed across teams.

### 2. Promotion raise: Recognizing a larger role

A promotion usually means a meaningful change in job level, decision-making scope, complexity or accountability. The pay adjustment should reflect the new role and the organization’s salary structure, not simply the employee’s performance rating in the old role.

A promotion and a merit increase can happen around the same time, but the organization should make the rationale for each component clear. Otherwise, it may be difficult to understand how much of the change reflects the new job and how much reflects performance in the previous role.

### 3. Cost-of-living adjustment: Responding to living-cost changes

A COLA is designed to respond to changes in the cost of living, sometimes using an inflation or consumer-price index. Employers do not all use the same approach. Some apply a broad adjustment; others make decisions based on location, policy, affordability or collective agreements.

A COLA is not the same as an individual performance reward. If an organization uses one, it should explain the index or reference, eligible population, timing and whether the adjustment is temporary or added to base salary.

### 4. Market adjustment: Addressing external competitiveness

A market adjustment responds to evidence that the pay for a particular job has moved relative to relevant external employers. It may be appropriate when a job family becomes difficult to recruit for or a required skill becomes more valuable in the labor market.

A market adjustment should be based on reliable, relevant market data and a documented comparison. A single recruiter conversation or one online salary estimate is rarely enough to justify a company-wide structural change.

### 5. Equity adjustment: Correcting an internal pay issue

An equity adjustment addresses a pay difference that cannot be adequately explained by consistent, job-related factors. It may emerge from a pay-equity audit, a salary-range review or a specific employee comparison. HR should examine the underlying facts and seek legal advice where appropriate.

### Comparison table: Which pay action fits?

| Pay action | Main reason | Typical evidence | Should it sit in the annual merit budget? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merit increase | Performance in current role | Performance assessment and policy | Usually, if the policy includes it |
| Promotion raise | Larger role or higher level | Approved role change and new range | Often separate or explicitly identified |
| COLA | Living-cost policy or index | Defined index, location and policy | Depends on the employer’s design |
| Market adjustment | External market movement | Relevant market data | Often a separate planning line |
| Equity adjustment | Internal fairness correction | Structured pay analysis | Usually separately tracked |
| Statutory adjustment | Legal wage requirement | Applicable law or regulation | Mandatory planning item where applicable |

### Why separating pay actions matters

Separate categories help leaders answer three important questions: What are we rewarding? What problem are we correcting? What will the action cost? If all adjustments are blended into one percentage, Finance may not know whether a budget increase reflects performance, promotions, market changes or compliance requirements.

It also helps employees understand the reason for a salary change. A promotion decision should not be explained as a generic merit reward, and a market correction should not imply that an employee’s recent performance suddenly changed.

### Frequently asked questions

**Can an employee receive both a promotion raise and a merit increase?**  
Yes, an organization may allow both, but it should define how the two decisions interact and prevent double-counting or inconsistent treatment.

**Is a cost-of-living increase the same as an annual raise?**  
No. An annual raise describes timing, while a COLA describes the purpose of the adjustment. An annual review may include merit, market, equity or cost-of-living components depending on company policy.

**Should market adjustments be included in merit budgets?**  
They can be included if the organization explicitly plans for them, but tracking them separately often gives Finance and HR better visibility into the cost of performance rewards versus market corrections.

**Is a merit increase guaranteed every year?**  
That depends on employment terms, local law and company policy. Do not assume that an annual review automatically creates an entitlement to an increase.
